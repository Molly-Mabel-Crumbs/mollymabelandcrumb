What Mabel should be doing after feeding

After you feed her, here's what to watch for — and what to do if things look off.

1–2 hours after feeding

You should see small bubbles forming

Tiny bubbles around the edges and through the starter mean fermentation has started. This is a good sign — Mabel is waking up. No bubbles at all after 2 hours? Your kitchen may be too cold — try moving her somewhere warmer like the top of the fridge or near the oven.

3–4 hours after feeding

She should be visibly rising

Mark the side of your jar with a rubber band or piece of tape right after feeding — this makes it easy to see exactly how much she's grown. At this point she should be noticeably higher than your mark. If she hasn't moved much, give her more time before worrying — temperature plays a huge role.

4–6 hours after feeding — the goal ✓

Peak rise — Mabel is ready to bake with

She should have doubled (or more), be domed or slightly domed on top, and smell yeasty and slightly tangy. This is peak — the best time to use her. Do the float test: drop a small spoonful into a glass of water. If it floats, she's ready. If it sinks, give her another 30–60 minutes and test again.

After peak — falling back down

Don't miss the window — but all is not lost

Once she starts falling she's past peak. Bread made with over-peaked starter can still be good but may be more sour and have less oven spring. If you missed it today, just feed her again and try tomorrow — don't use a starter that has fully collapsed and smells very acidic.

Pro tips for a stronger starter

🌾 Slow to rise? Add a little whole wheat flour

Whole wheat flour has more wild yeast and nutrients than white flour, which gives your starter a real boost. Try replacing 20–30% of your feed flour with whole wheat (e.g. 15g whole wheat + 35g bread flour instead of 50g bread flour). You'll likely notice a faster, more active rise within a feed or two.

🌡️ Temperature is everything

A starter at 75–80°F peaks in 4–6 hours. The same starter at 65°F might take 8–10 hours. If your kitchen is cold in winter, your starter isn't broken — she's just slow. A reliable warm spot: on top of your fridge, inside your oven with just the light on, or next to a warm appliance.

💧 Use lukewarm water, not cold

Cold tap water slows fermentation significantly. Use water that's around 75–80°F — comfortable to the touch but not hot. If your tap water is heavily chlorinated, let it sit in an open container for an hour first or use filtered water.

📏 Mark your jar every time

A rubber band or piece of masking tape at the starting level after each feed makes it so easy to track rise. You can see at a glance if she's doubled, and you'll start to learn her rhythm — how fast she rises, when she peaks, when she starts to fall. This is how you get consistent bakes.

🫙 Don't seal the jar completely

Your starter produces CO₂ as it ferments — it needs to breathe. Use a loose lid, a cloth cover, or a jar lid set on top without locking. A sealed jar can build pressure and slow things down (or make a mess when you open it!).

Always keep some Mabel back 🫙 Never use all of your starter in a bake. Always keep at least 20–30g back so you can feed her again. The amounts above already account for this — the "use in dough" amount leaves Mabel with enough to carry on to your next bake.
